Chapter 6

The Trap of Playing God

Releasing the Need to Make Others Pay

Key Scriptures: Romans 12:17–21; Genesis 45:1–8; Genesis 50:19–21; 1 Samuel 24:1–12; Psalm 73:1–28; Proverbs 4:23; Acts 25:10–11.

Central Truth

Trusting God with judgment does not require passivity in the face of wrongdoing. It means pursuing truth, protection, and appropriate accountability without allowing personal vengeance or the desire to control another person’s fate to govern our hearts.

When Justice Becomes Personal

There is a moment in the journey of betrayal when the heart begins to wrestle with a question that is difficult to admit aloud: What if I never see them face the consequences of what they did? It is a question born from the tension between the desire for justice and the pain of watching someone continue with life as though nothing happened. When we have been mistreated, betrayed, falsely accused, manipulated, or wounded by people who refuse to acknowledge their wrongdoing, it can be deeply frustrating to see them move forward while we are still trying to recover from the damage they caused.

We may watch them maintain their friendships, continue serving in ministry, enjoy the approval of others, or receive opportunities that we believe their conduct should have disqualified them from receiving. Meanwhile, we are left carrying questions, rebuilding our confidence, and trying to understand how something so painful could happen without any visible acknowledgment of responsibility. We may begin to wonder whether truth matters, whether anyone will ever recognize what happened, and whether justice is simply reserved for people who have enough influence to demand it.

These are not small questions. Scripture does not instruct us to become indifferent toward wrongdoing. God cares about truth, righteousness, the protection of the vulnerable, and the accountability of those who exercise power. The Bible repeatedly confronts dishonesty, oppression, partiality, and the abuse of authority. We should never interpret trust in God as permission to ignore injustice or to abandon people who need protection.

Yet there is a dangerous turning point when the pursuit of justice becomes entangled with the personal need to make another person suffer. We begin to think that our healing will not be complete until the offender loses what they have, that our peace cannot return until their reputation collapses, or that our testimony will not be vindicated until everyone who doubted us admits that we were right.

At that point, the offender may no longer be the only person making destructive choices. The offense has begun to dictate the terms of our future, and we have begun to measure our freedom by what happens to someone else.

This is the trap of playing God.

We do not literally believe that we possess God’s authority, but our actions can reveal that we have taken upon ourselves the responsibility of determining what another person deserves, how their punishment should unfold, and when they should finally suffer for what they have done. We begin keeping an account of their failures, watching for signs of their downfall, and interpreting every disappointment in their life as evidence that judgment has finally arrived. We may even feel disappointed when they appear to prosper, as though their apparent success somehow invalidates our pain.

But another person’s visible circumstances are not a reliable measurement of God’s judgment. We do not know everything occurring within their life, and we are not qualified to declare the meaning of every blessing, loss, opportunity, or consequence they experience. Our responsibility is not to monitor another person’s life until we are satisfied that they have paid enough. Our responsibility is to remain faithful to God, pursue what is right, and refuse to surrender our hearts to vengeance.

The question is not whether wrongdoing should be addressed. It should. The question is whether we will allow our pursuit of accountability to become an obsession with controlling another person’s fate.

The Difference Between Justice and Vengeance

Justice and vengeance can look similar from a distance because both may involve consequences for wrongdoing. However, their purposes are different, and that difference matters greatly in the life of a believer.

Justice seeks to establish truth, protect people from harm, hold individuals accountable for their conduct, and pursue consequences that are appropriate to the offense. It requires discernment, evidence, fairness, and a willingness to acknowledge facts even when those facts challenge our assumptions. Justice does not depend upon our personal satisfaction with the outcome. It seeks what is right, not merely what feels emotionally rewarding.

Vengeance, by contrast, seeks personal repayment. It wants the offender to experience suffering because we have suffered. It may begin with a legitimate desire for accountability, but gradually the focus shifts from correcting wrongdoing to making the wrongdoer feel our pain. We may no longer be satisfied with an appropriate consequence because we want the person to lose everything. We may no longer be content for the truth to be established because we want the person publicly humiliated. We may no longer be seeking protection or restoration because we want to witness their downfall.

The distinction is not always obvious to us. We can sincerely believe that we are pursuing justice while allowing resentment to influence our expectations. That is why we must examine our motives honestly, without using that examination to dismiss legitimate grievances or excuse wrongdoing.

Consider a person who reports misconduct by a leader. The report may be necessary to protect others, establish the facts, and ensure accountability. Seeking a fair investigation is not vengeance simply because the investigation may have serious consequences for the leader. The same is true when someone reports a crime, documents harassment, seeks a protective order, or pursues legal remedies. Appropriate accountability is not inherently unchristian.

However, if the purpose of the process gradually becomes the desire to destroy the person’s family, humiliate them beyond what the facts warrant, or ensure that they never experience another good day, then the heart needs examination. We can insist that wrongdoing be addressed without making another person’s total ruin the objective of our lives.

Romans 12:19 teaches believers not to avenge themselves but to leave room for the judgment of God. This instruction is not a command to become passive in the face of evil. It is a command to recognize the limits of our authority and refuse to make personal retaliation our governing principle.

There is room for justice without hatred. There is room for consequences without cruelty. There is room for truth without a campaign of humiliation. There is room for a firm boundary without the secret desire to punish someone every day for the rest of their life.

When justice is pursued responsibly, we can accept that the process must be guided by truth and fairness, even when the outcome differs from what we expected. When vengeance takes control, no outcome seems sufficient because the real objective is no longer justice. It is satisfaction through another person’s suffering.

God Is the Judge, Not Our Personal Instrument of Revenge

Throughout Scripture, God reveals Himself as righteous and just. His authority is not limited by human influence, public opinion, or the ability of powerful people to conceal their actions. He sees what people do, knows what is hidden from human understanding, and judges with a knowledge that no human investigator or observer possesses in its entirety.

This truth should bring both comfort and humility.

It brings comfort because injustice does not become invisible merely because people refuse to acknowledge it. A person may successfully manage a public reputation while refusing to take responsibility in private, but the appearance of success does not make wrongdoing righteous. God is not dependent upon public approval to know the truth.

It also brings humility because God’s judgment is not an instrument we control. We cannot command Him to punish someone according to our preferred timetable, and we cannot assume that every hardship another person experiences is evidence that God is retaliating on our behalf. We must be careful about declaring divine judgment over people simply because they have hurt us.

Sometimes believers say, “God is going to get them,” when what they really mean is, “I cannot rest until they suffer.” The words may sound spiritual, but the desire underneath them may be rooted in personal vengeance rather than trust. We must not use the language of God’s justice to baptize our resentment.

Trusting God with judgment means acknowledging that we do not possess complete knowledge. We know what we experienced, what we observed, and what can be established through evidence. We may have legitimate concerns about what happened and may be justified in pursuing accountability. But we do not know every motive, every circumstance, or every aspect of another person’s heart. Our understanding is limited, and our judgments must remain accountable to truth.

God’s justice is not weakened by our refusal to seek revenge. We do not have to keep hatred alive to prove that the offense mattered. We do not have to monitor the offender’s life to ensure that God has remembered what happened. We do not have to make ourselves the final authority over the person’s future.

Our responsibility is to do what is right with the knowledge and authority we have been given. God’s responsibility is not ours to assume.

This does not mean that we should sit quietly and wait for heaven to resolve every earthly problem. God can work through truthful testimony, responsible leaders, lawful authorities, safeguarding measures, and people who are willing to confront wrongdoing. Seeking help and taking action can be entirely consistent with trusting Him.

The difference is that we take action because something needs to be made right, not because we have appointed ourselves to control the ultimate fate of another person.

When We Want God to Prove That We Were Right

One of the most painful parts of betrayal is being misunderstood. It is difficult enough to experience wrongdoing, but the burden can become heavier when others believe the offender’s version of events, question our character, or refuse to hear our concerns. In those circumstances, the desire for vindication can become overwhelming.

We want the truth to be known. We want the people who judged us unfairly to reconsider their conclusions. We want those who dismissed our concerns to recognize that we were not imagining what happened. We want the people who defended the wrong behavior to acknowledge that their response was mistaken.

These desires are understandable. False accusations and unjust treatment can have real consequences for employment, family relationships, ministry opportunities, friendships, and emotional well-being. A person’s reputation can be damaged by misinformation, and a responsible effort to correct the record may be necessary.

But there is a difference between seeking to correct a false account and making universal recognition the condition of our peace.

We may never convince every person of our perspective. Some people will remain committed to what they already believe. Others may lack the information necessary to understand the situation. Some may be unwilling to reconsider their conclusions, even when presented with credible evidence. We cannot force every observer to become fair, thoughtful, or willing to admit error.

If our peace depends upon persuading every person who misunderstood us, we may spend years attempting to control opinions that are not ours to control. We may repeatedly explain ourselves to people who have no intention of listening, revisit conflicts that cannot be resolved through further conversation, and allow our lives to revolve around correcting every misrepresentation.

There are times when a clear response is necessary. There are times when legal advice, formal documentation, a workplace process, or an appropriate public correction may be warranted. We should not confuse responsible self-advocacy with pride or assume that silence is always the most spiritual response.

However, once we have taken reasonable steps to establish the truth, we must consider whether continued argument is accomplishing anything constructive. Sometimes we have provided the relevant facts, made our concerns known, and fulfilled our responsibilities. Continuing to fight for universal approval may only deepen our exhaustion.

Jesus did not entrust His identity to the changing opinions of the crowd. He remained faithful to the Father even when people misunderstood Him, rejected Him, and spoke falsely about Him. His example does not mean that every accusation must go unanswered. It reminds us that our ultimate identity and calling cannot be built entirely upon the approval of people who may misunderstand us.

You can correct the record without making vindication your life’s mission. You can tell the truth without begging every listener to believe you. You can grieve the loss of a reputation or relationship while refusing to let the opinions of others determine your worth.

Sometimes freedom begins when we stop asking every person who misunderstood us to become the judge of our value.

The Hidden Cost of Watching for Their Downfall

When someone has hurt us deeply, it can become tempting to watch their life closely. We may pay attention to their relationships, employment, ministry, public reputation, and personal circumstances, searching for signs that the consequences we believe they deserve are finally arriving. If they experience a setback, we may interpret it as proof that justice is taking place. If they appear to prosper, we may become angry or discouraged.

This pattern can consume far more of our attention than we realize.

Every new development becomes another opportunity to revisit the offense. Every success becomes another question about why justice has not occurred. Every failure becomes an invitation to feel vindicated. The offender remains at the center of our emotional world even when we have no meaningful relationship with them anymore.

We may tell ourselves that we are simply waiting for justice, but we have begun to organize our attention around another person’s life. We are no longer merely responding to what happened. We are allowing the possibility of their punishment to determine our emotional condition.

That is an expensive way to live.

The time spent monitoring another person’s circumstances is time that could be invested in our own recovery, our family, our work, our spiritual growth, and the relationships that are still capable of flourishing. The energy devoted to imagining their humiliation could be used to seek wise counsel, develop healthy boundaries, rebuild confidence, or pursue meaningful goals.

This does not mean that we should ignore developments that affect our safety or a legitimate accountability process. If an investigation is ongoing, a legal matter requires attention, or a person continues to pose a risk, monitoring relevant information may be necessary. The issue is not responsible awareness. The issue is emotional fixation that continues even when it no longer serves a constructive purpose.

Proverbs 4:23 instructs us to guard our hearts because the course of our lives flows from them. Guarding the heart includes paying attention to what we repeatedly entertain, what we permit to shape our attitudes, and what we allow to consume our attention.

We cannot always prevent a thought or memory from appearing, but we can learn to decide whether we will continue feeding it. We can recognize when we have begun searching for updates that only reopen the wound. We can step away from unnecessary exposure, stop seeking information that serves no practical purpose, and redirect our attention toward responsibilities and relationships that deserve our care.

The goal is not to become indifferent to justice. It is to stop making another person’s downfall the condition of our well-being.

Their life is not your assignment. Your healing, your integrity, and your faithfulness are responsibilities you can begin addressing today.

When the Wicked Seem to Prosper

One of the oldest struggles of faith is the observation that people who behave unjustly sometimes appear to prosper while those who seek to live righteously endure suffering. Scripture does not avoid this tension. The psalmists wrestle with it openly, demonstrating that believers throughout history have struggled to reconcile what they observe with their understanding of God’s justice.

Psalm 73 is especially relevant. Asaph describes his distress when he sees the prosperity of the wicked and begins to question whether his commitment to righteousness has been worthwhile. His struggle is not presented as an insignificant failure of faith. It is a serious spiritual crisis brought about by the apparent contradiction between what he believes and what he sees.

The psalm’s movement is important. Asaph does not resolve his struggle by pretending that injustice is imaginary. Instead, his understanding changes as he considers the larger reality of God’s presence, justice, and ultimate purposes. He begins to recognize that appearances do not provide the complete picture.

This is a necessary lesson for anyone who has watched an offender appear to move forward without visible consequences.

A person’s public success does not establish their moral innocence. Their influence does not prove that God approves of their conduct. Their continued opportunities do not demonstrate that the people they harmed were unimportant. Human circumstances are too complex for us to draw such conclusions with confidence.

At the same time, we must resist using every positive development in another person’s life as evidence that God has forgotten us. God’s care for us is not measured by whether someone who hurt us loses a job, experiences a failed relationship, or faces public embarrassment. Our value is not increased by their suffering, and our suffering is not made meaningless by their apparent success.

If we begin measuring God’s faithfulness through the misfortune of our enemies, we create a spiritual dependence upon events that we cannot control. We may find ourselves unable to rejoice in our own lives because we are still waiting for someone else to experience a corresponding loss.

The goodness of God does not need another person’s humiliation to become real.

We can grieve injustice and still recognize the blessings that remain in our lives. We can ask God to intervene while continuing to build what is good. We can pursue accountability without assuming that a visible punishment is the only evidence that truth matters.

Sometimes we may see consequences. Sometimes we may not see them in the way we expected. In either case, we are called to remain faithful rather than allowing our spiritual lives to become a running comparison between our circumstances and those of the people who hurt us.

The question is not whether the offender appears to be prospering. The question is whether we will continue to trust God and do what is right, even when the visible circumstances do not provide the resolution we desire.

Joseph: Refusing to Become What Hurt You

The life of Joseph offers a powerful example of the tension between injustice, responsibility, and the temptation to repay those who have caused harm. In Genesis 37, Joseph’s brothers betray him, cast him into a pit, and sell him into slavery. Their actions are not a misunderstanding or a minor disagreement. They fundamentally alter the course of his life and separate him from his family.

Joseph subsequently experiences years of hardship, including false accusation and imprisonment. The biblical account does not suggest that his suffering was insignificant or that his brothers’ behavior should be excused. Their actions were serious, and the consequences affected multiple lives.

Eventually, circumstances change. Joseph rises to a position of authority in Egypt, and a famine brings his brothers before him in need of food. The brothers who once held power over Joseph’s immediate future now stand before a man who possesses the authority to determine what happens to them.

Imagine the emotional weight of that moment. Joseph has the opportunity to confront the people responsible for his suffering from a position of power they never expected him to possess. He could have used his authority to humiliate them, deny them assistance, or inflict suffering in return. Instead, the story develops toward recognition, testing, revelation, and eventual reconciliation.

Joseph does not immediately reveal his identity, and the process includes difficult interactions. His response is not a simplistic picture of instant emotional resolution. The account allows us to see that the restoration of a broken family can involve the exposure of truth and the demonstration of changed behavior.

In Genesis 45, Joseph eventually identifies himself to his brothers and acknowledges the larger providence of God in the circumstances that brought him to Egypt. Later, in Genesis 50:20, he tells them that although they intended evil against him, God worked through the circumstances to preserve life.

This statement does not transform his brothers’ intentions into something good. Joseph does not declare that selling him into slavery was acceptable because a positive outcome eventually emerged. Rather, he recognizes that human wrongdoing did not prevent God from accomplishing a purpose beyond the intentions of those who harmed him.

That distinction is essential.

We should never tell a wounded person that the harm they experienced was necessary or good simply because something meaningful may eventually emerge from their recovery. God can bring growth, wisdom, compassion, and renewed purpose out of painful circumstances without requiring us to call the wrongdoing itself righteous.

Joseph’s story also reminds us that having the power to retaliate does not mean retaliation is the right response. His position gives him opportunities to choose how he will exercise authority. He does not have to become a mirror image of the people who harmed him.

The lesson is not that every betrayal will end in a restored relationship or that every offender will eventually confess. Our circumstances may not follow Joseph’s story. The lesson is that the wrong choices of others do not have to determine the character of our own choices.

You can be wounded without becoming cruel. You can be given authority without using it to humiliate. You can remember what happened without allowing it to become permission to harm others.

The person who hurt you does not get to decide what kind of person you become.

When God Gives You the Opportunity to Retaliate

Sometimes the greatest test comes when we finally have the opportunity to give someone a taste of their own behavior. The person who once dismissed us may now need our help. The individual who spread damaging information may become dependent upon our cooperation. Someone who once used their position against us may find themselves in a situation where we possess influence.

In those moments, our intentions become especially important.

We may tell ourselves that we are simply restoring balance. We may believe that the other person deserves to experience the same humiliation, exclusion, or fear they once caused. We may even convince ourselves that allowing them to suffer will teach them a lesson they otherwise would never learn.

But there is a danger in treating our power as an opportunity to settle personal accounts. If we use authority to punish someone outside appropriate processes, manipulate circumstances to damage their reputation, or deliberately withhold something necessary because we want them to suffer, we risk reproducing the very behavior we condemned.

The fact that another person acted unjustly does not grant us unlimited permission to act unjustly in return.

David faced this kind of test in 1 Samuel 24. While being pursued by Saul, David had an opportunity to kill the king in a cave. David’s circumstances were difficult, and Saul’s treatment of him had placed his life in danger. Yet David refused to take the opportunity to kill Saul. He recognized that the opportunity itself did not establish that the action would be right.

David’s decision does not mean that he had to pretend Saul’s conduct was acceptable or that he was required to place himself indefinitely in danger. David continued to act with caution and did not simply return to an unsafe relationship. The account shows that refusing personal vengeance and protecting oneself are not necessarily incompatible.

There will be moments when we must decide whether we are using our power responsibly or using it to satisfy resentment. A supervisor may need to address an employee’s misconduct, but the response should be based on evidence and fair policy rather than a personal desire for humiliation. A ministry leader may need to correct harmful behavior, but discipline should not become a weapon for settling old disputes. A family member may need to establish a boundary, but the boundary should be designed to protect well-being rather than to manipulate another person into suffering.

The question to ask is not merely, “Do they deserve this?” The question is, “Is this action truthful, proportionate, responsible, and consistent with what is right?”

We may not control what another person chooses to do, but we remain responsible for what we choose to do with the authority entrusted to us.

Do not let the opportunity to retaliate become the moment when you abandon the principles you claimed to defend.

Forgiveness Does Not Require You to Protect Wrongdoing

There is an important balance that must remain clear throughout this discussion: releasing vengeance is not the same as refusing to act.

Some people have been taught that trusting God means allowing every injustice to unfold without intervention. They may fear that reporting misconduct, pursuing legal action, correcting misinformation, or establishing firm boundaries would reveal a lack of forgiveness. This misunderstanding can leave vulnerable people exposed to further harm and allow those who misuse authority to continue without meaningful accountability.

That is not the message of this chapter.

The Bible contains examples of people who spoke against wrongdoing, appealed to legitimate authorities, and refused to participate in unjust actions. In Acts 25:10–11, Paul appeals to Caesar when facing legal proceedings. His appeal is not presented as a rejection of faith. It is a practical use of the legal rights available to him within his circumstances.

Likewise, Proverbs repeatedly emphasizes honest judgment, protection of the vulnerable, and the responsibility to speak when silence would allow injustice to continue. Christians should not interpret forgiveness as a prohibition against truthful reporting or appropriate protective measures.

If a person has harmed a child, threatened someone’s safety, committed a crime, or abused a position of authority, the appropriate response may require immediate protective action and formal reporting. The exact steps depend on the circumstances and applicable law, but forgiveness should never be used to pressure someone into concealing danger.

There may also be workplace or church situations in which documentation, formal complaints, independent review, or legal advice are necessary. Taking such steps does not mean that we are refusing to trust God. It may mean that we are acting responsibly with the resources and authority available to us.

What we must examine is the purpose and manner of our response. Are we pursuing a truthful process that addresses harm and protects people? Are we willing to accept a fair evaluation of the evidence? Are we committed to appropriate accountability rather than demanding punishment regardless of the facts? Are we acting to prevent harm rather than using the situation as an excuse for personal destruction?

These questions do not weaken our commitment to justice. They help keep our pursuit of justice grounded in integrity.

Forgiveness releases the claim to personal vengeance. It does not erase the responsibility to protect people, report serious wrongdoing, or address conduct that must not continue.

We can surrender our desire to control another person’s ultimate judgment while still taking the practical steps necessary to confront what is wrong.

A Prophetic Call to the Church: Accountability Is Not Vengeance

The Church must be especially careful when discussing judgment, forgiveness, and authority because these subjects can be used either to promote spiritual maturity or to protect unhealthy systems.

Sometimes a person who raises a legitimate concern is accused of being bitter simply because the concern may have consequences for someone in leadership. Sometimes a request for accountability is described as an attack on the anointing. Sometimes the language of forgiveness is used to pressure a wounded person into silence before the facts have been examined.

These responses do not establish holiness. They can prevent the very accountability that healthy Christian leadership requires.

No title, ministry gift, public reputation, or history of service places a person beyond the responsibility to tell the truth and answer for their conduct. Leaders are human beings who need grace, but grace does not remove accountability. In fact, leadership increases the importance of responsible conduct because decisions made by people in authority can affect many lives.

A church that takes holiness seriously must be willing to examine misconduct fairly. It must protect people who raise concerns in good faith, avoid retaliation, consider evidence carefully, and take appropriate action when wrongdoing is substantiated. Serious matters may require independent oversight or reporting to the relevant authorities rather than being handled solely within the circle of people who have an interest in protecting the institution.

At the same time, accountability must not become a license for public cruelty, unsupported accusations, or punishment based on rumor. A fair process should protect both those who may have been harmed and those who are accused from unjust treatment. Truth must be established through responsible means, not through a contest over who can mobilize the greatest number of supporters.

The Church must reject both concealment and vengeance. Concealment protects wrongdoing from examination. Vengeance abandons fairness in pursuit of personal satisfaction. Neither reflects the full character of biblical justice.

We must learn to say that forgiveness is necessary without suggesting that consequences are forbidden. We must be able to call for accountability without assuming that our purpose is to destroy another person. We must create a culture in which repentance involves more than words and where those who have caused harm are encouraged to take responsibility, make appropriate amends, and demonstrate meaningful change.

If someone has abused authority, the answer is not to abandon all respect for authority. It is to insist that authority be exercised responsibly. If a leader has failed, the answer is not to assume that every leader is corrupt. It is to examine the failure, protect those affected, and make the changes necessary to prevent further harm.

The Church does not honor God by pretending that leaders are incapable of wrongdoing. It honors Him by submitting every form of human authority to truth, righteousness, humility, and accountability.

A prophetic call to the Church must therefore challenge the wounded to release personal vengeance while challenging leaders and institutions to stop using forgiveness as a shield against responsibility. Both calls belong together.

The Freedom of Leaving Judgment in God’s Hands

Releasing judgment to God can sound simple until we begin to consider what we are being asked to surrender. We may be surrendering the hope of seeing an offender publicly exposed. We may be surrendering the fantasy of delivering the perfect speech that finally makes them understand our pain. We may be surrendering the expectation that everyone who misunderstood us will one day admit that we were right.

These are difficult losses because they can become part of the story we tell ourselves about how healing will happen. We imagine that one day the offender will apologize, the people who doubted us will recognize the truth, and the entire situation will finally make sense. We picture a moment in which everything that was hidden becomes visible and everyone receives the understanding they deserve.

Sometimes circumstances do lead to acknowledgment, correction, or reconciliation. When that happens, we can be grateful. But we cannot build our spiritual freedom around an outcome that depends upon decisions outside our control.

Leaving judgment in God’s hands means acknowledging that we are not responsible for controlling every consequence. We are responsible for acting with integrity, speaking truthfully, pursuing appropriate remedies, and maintaining the boundaries necessary for safety and well-being. We are not responsible for manufacturing another person’s repentance or arranging their punishment.

This surrender may need to be practiced repeatedly. When the memory returns, we may need to remind ourselves that we have already chosen not to pursue revenge. When new information emerges, we may need to determine whether a responsible action is necessary. When we discover that the offender appears to be prospering, we may need to resist the temptation to interpret that fact as evidence that justice has failed.

Trusting God does not require us to ignore what is happening. It requires us to distinguish between what calls for our responsible action and what we cannot control.

There is freedom in recognizing that we do not have to carry the burden of being the final judge of another person’s life. We can stop treating every new development as a verdict on our own worth. We can stop allowing our peace to rise and fall according to whether the offender appears to be suffering. We can redirect our attention toward the life God has placed before us.

We may never know the full outcome of every conflict. We may never receive all the answers we desire. But we can still live with integrity, build meaningful relationships, pursue our calling, and find joy that is not dependent upon another person’s misfortune.

The release of vengeance does not mean that we have stopped caring about justice. It means that justice is no longer an excuse to surrender our entire lives to resentment.

Build a Future That Does Not Depend on Their Failure

One of the clearest signs of growing freedom is the ability to imagine a meaningful future that does not depend upon the failure of the person who hurt us.

At first, that future may be difficult to picture. The offense may have affected our confidence, disrupted our relationships, or changed the direction of our lives. We may have lost opportunities, endured financial consequences, or experienced a painful separation from a community that once mattered deeply to us. It would be insensitive to suggest that such consequences can be undone by a simple change in attitude.

Some losses require practical rebuilding. Some relationships cannot be restored. Some consequences remain long after the original event. We should acknowledge these realities rather than promising that everything will return to the way it was before.

Yet even when our circumstances cannot be restored exactly as they were, there may still be meaningful work ahead of us. We can develop new skills, build healthier relationships, pursue a different calling, seek appropriate support, and discover forms of service that reflect what we have learned. We can become more discerning without becoming suspicious of everyone. We can become more compassionate without tolerating mistreatment. We can become more committed to accountability without making conflict the center of our identity.

The future does not need to be built around proving that the past was unfair. The injustice may already be clear, and the consequences may already be real. We do not need to spend every remaining year trying to make other people recognize what we have experienced before we allow ourselves to grow.

There is a difference between learning from a painful chapter and allowing it to become the entire book.

Perhaps the person who hurt you will never acknowledge what happened. Perhaps the institution that failed you will never make the changes you hoped to see. Perhaps some people will continue believing a version of events that you know is incomplete or false. Those realities may continue to cause grief, but they do not remove your ability to make meaningful decisions about the life ahead of you.

You can pursue the responsibilities that remain before you. You can invest in people who demonstrate honesty and care. You can return to work that gives you purpose, deepen your understanding of Scripture, develop your gifts, and contribute to the well-being of others. You can make decisions based on your values rather than on the need to prove something to the people who hurt you.

None of these steps means that the past no longer matters. They mean that the past is no longer the only thing that matters.

Do not wait for another person’s failure before you permit yourself to flourish. Do not require the collapse of someone else’s reputation before you rebuild your own life. Do not make your future dependent upon an apology that may never come.

Let your life become evidence that wrongdoing did not have the final word.

Chapter Conclusion: You Are Not the Judge of Their Final Chapter

The desire for justice is understandable, especially when we have experienced harm that has not been acknowledged or addressed. We should not dismiss that desire or confuse it with bitterness in every circumstance. Truth matters, accountability matters, and the protection of vulnerable people matters. The Christian life does not require us to remain passive when wrongdoing must be confronted.

But we must also recognize the danger of allowing our pursuit of justice to become a personal campaign of vengeance. When we begin monitoring another person’s life for signs of punishment, measuring our peace by their suffering, or making their humiliation the condition of our healing, we have allowed the offense to take a place in our lives that it was never meant to occupy.

We are called to pursue what is right, not to appoint ourselves as the final authority over another person’s destiny.

The story of Joseph reminds us that the wrongdoing of others does not have to determine the character of our future. The example of David reminds us that opportunities for retaliation do not automatically make retaliation righteous. The prayers of the psalmists remind us that we can bring our questions and frustrations honestly before God. The teaching of Paul reminds us that we can refuse personal vengeance while continuing to do what is right.

These truths belong together. We can forgive without excusing. We can establish boundaries without hatred. We can pursue accountability without cruelty. We can speak the truth without making humiliation our purpose. We can trust God with judgment without abandoning our responsibility to protect people and confront wrongdoing.

If you have been hurt, you do not need to pretend that the injustice was insignificant. You do not need to silence legitimate concerns or accept continued mistreatment. You can take appropriate action, seek help, and insist upon responsible processes. But you do not have to spend the rest of your life waiting for the offender to experience the exact pain you experienced.

Their repentance is their responsibility. Their consequences are not yours to manufacture. Their public reputation is not the measure of your worth. Your calling is not to supervise their downfall.

Your calling is to remain faithful to God and responsible for your own conduct.

There may be things you never understand and outcomes you never witness. There may be apologies that never arrive and people who never admit that they were wrong. You may have to grieve those realities while continuing to pursue truth and healing. But you can do so without becoming consumed by the need to control what happens next in someone else’s life.

Release the demand to be the judge of their final chapter. Take responsibility for the chapter God has placed before you. Pursue justice where justice is needed, seek healing where healing is needed, and establish boundaries where boundaries are necessary. Then allow yourself to build a future that is not dependent upon the failure of another person.

You are not required to make someone else suffer in order for your suffering to matter. You are not required to destroy another person’s reputation in order for the truth to be important. You are not required to control another person’s fate in order to reclaim your own life.

God’s justice does not need your vengeance to remain just.

Let that truth free you from the exhausting responsibility of trying to make everyone pay. Let it restore your attention to the life you can still build. Let it remind you that you can pursue what is right without becoming consumed by resentment.

You may not be the judge of their final chapter, but you are still responsible for how you live yours.

Chapter 6 Study Guide: The Trap of Playing God

Group Discussion Questions

  1. What is the difference between pursuing justice and pursuing vengeance?
  2. Why can it become tempting to measure our healing by whether the person who hurt us experiences consequences?
  3. How does Joseph’s response to his brothers demonstrate that we do not have to reproduce the behavior that harmed us?
  4. What can David’s decision in 1 Samuel 24 teach us about opportunities to retaliate?
  5. How can believers trust God with judgment while still reporting misconduct or pursuing appropriate legal and institutional remedies?
  6. What are the dangers of using spiritual language to declare that every hardship experienced by an offender is divine punishment?
  7. How can the Church protect both people who report concerns in good faith and people who deserve a fair evaluation of allegations?
  8. What is the difference between seeking to correct misinformation and becoming consumed by the need to prove ourselves to everyone?
  9. How can a person recognize when following an offender’s life has become an unhealthy emotional fixation rather than a necessary part of an accountability process?
  10. What practical changes could help someone build a future that is not dependent upon another person’s apology, punishment, or public humiliation?

Personal Reflection

Consider whether an unresolved offense has caused you to focus more attention on another person’s circumstances than on the responsibilities and opportunities in your own life. This reflection is not intended to minimize the harm you may have experienced. It is an opportunity to identify what remains within your control and where you may need support.

Ask yourself:

  • Am I pursuing a legitimate form of accountability, or have I begun seeking personal repayment?
  • What do I hope will happen to the person who hurt me, and why?
  • Would I still pursue the appropriate outcome if I knew that I might never receive a public apology or personal acknowledgment?
  • Is there a practical action I need to take to protect myself or others?
  • Have I begun interpreting another person’s success or hardship as a verdict on my own worth?
  • What would it mean to entrust judgment to God without abandoning my responsibilities?
  • What part of my future have I delayed while waiting for someone else to face consequences?

Write your answers honestly. If there is an ongoing legal, workplace, family, or safeguarding concern, consider seeking appropriate guidance rather than relying solely on private reflection or prayer.

Personal Application for the Week

Choose one action that helps you redirect your attention toward what you can responsibly influence. You may need to take a necessary step toward accountability, establish a boundary, stop seeking unnecessary updates about the person who hurt you, correct a false statement through an appropriate process, or invest time in a relationship or responsibility that has been neglected.

If an active safety concern or formal process requires you to remain informed, continue doing so responsibly. The goal is not to ignore relevant information. It is to stop feeding a fixation that no longer serves truth, protection, or healing.

At the end of the week, reflect on whether you have been able to separate your pursuit of what is right from your desire to control another person’s outcome. Ask God to help you remain committed to truth while releasing the burden of personal vengeance.

Closing Prayer

Lord Jesus, I bring before You the people who have hurt me and the circumstances that have left me searching for justice. You know what happened, what it cost me, and the questions that remain unanswered. You know the moments when I have wanted the truth to be recognized and the times when I have wondered whether those responsible will ever face the consequences of their actions.

Give me wisdom to distinguish between pursuing justice and seeking revenge. Help me recognize when I need to speak, when I need to seek appropriate accountability, and when I am attempting to control something that does not belong to me. Do not allow my desire for justice to become a desire for cruelty. Do not allow my pain to become permission to act in ways that violate the truth I claim to defend.

I ask You to protect those who are vulnerable, expose wrongdoing through truthful and responsible means, and give courage to those who must speak against injustice. Strengthen leaders who are willing to act with humility and integrity. Give wisdom to those responsible for investigating concerns, and help them pursue fair processes without favoritism or retaliation.

Where I have been misunderstood, help me know when a correction is necessary and when I must stop seeking approval from people who may never understand. Where my reputation has been damaged, guide me toward appropriate ways to address the harm. Where I have suffered losses that cannot be restored, comfort me and help me recognize the possibilities that remain before me.

Lord, forgive me for the times I have wanted to control another person’s punishment or have found satisfaction in imagining their downfall. I acknowledge that I do not possess Your complete knowledge or authority. Help me entrust judgment to You without becoming passive toward wrongdoing. Teach me to take responsible action while refusing to surrender my heart to vengeance.

Give me the strength to follow the example of Joseph, who did not allow the betrayal of his brothers to determine the character of his future. Give me the discernment to recognize, as David did, that an opportunity to retaliate is not automatically permission to do so. Help me remain faithful when the circumstances appear unfair and when the outcome does not arrive according to my expectations.

I place before You the apologies I may never receive, the recognition I may never experience, and the consequences I may never witness. I ask You to free me from the exhausting responsibility of trying to make everyone pay. Restore my attention to the responsibilities, relationships, and opportunities You have placed before me.

Help me pursue justice with integrity, forgiveness with wisdom, and peace without denying the truth. Let my life be shaped by Your character rather than by the wrongdoing of others. Teach me to build a future that does not depend upon another person’s failure.

I entrust the things I cannot control to You, and I accept responsibility for the choices I can make today. Lead me toward a life of truth, courage, mercy, and spiritual freedom.

In the name of Jesus, Amen.
