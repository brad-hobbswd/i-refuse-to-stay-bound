The Decision to Walk in Freedom

The beginning of the journey

The Decision to Walk in Freedom

There comes a moment in the life of every believer when the Word of God must become more than something we read, teach, preach, or hear spoken from a pulpit, because there are seasons when the greatest test of our faith is not found in what we know about God, but in whether we are willing to trust Him with the places in our hearts that have been wounded by the actions of other people. It is one thing to declare that Jesus Christ has the power to set the captives free when we are standing in a sanctuary surrounded by people who agree with us, but it is another thing entirely to believe that freedom is still possible when we have experienced betrayal, endured rejection, watched relationships fall apart, or discovered that the people we trusted were capable of causing the very pain we once believed they would help us overcome.

Some wounds leave marks that are visible to everyone around us, while others remain hidden beneath our smiles, our worship, our service, and the responsibilities we continue to carry because life does not always give us the opportunity to stop and process what has happened. We may continue attending church, singing songs of praise, teaching lessons, serving in ministry, encouraging others, and speaking words of faith while privately wrestling with questions we are afraid to ask aloud. We may wonder how someone who spoke so passionately about the love of God could treat another person with such disregard, why honesty sometimes appears to be punished while silence is rewarded, or why those who have been hurt are occasionally expected to carry the responsibility of restoring peace while those who caused the damage are allowed to continue without examination. When these questions remain unanswered, the pain can settle into places within us that we never intended to surrender, gradually affecting our ability to trust, our willingness to love, and our understanding of what it means to walk in the freedom of Jesus Christ.

This book begins with a conviction that must remain at the center of everything we discuss: what happened to you may be part of your story, but it does not have to become the authority over your future. The betrayal may have been real, the rejection may have been undeserved, and the consequences may have reached further into your life than anyone else understands, but the wrong committed against you does not have to determine the condition of your heart for the remainder of your days. God does not ask us to deny the truth in order to demonstrate faith, nor does He require us to pretend that wrongdoing never occurred so that others can remain comfortable with their own decisions. Instead, He calls us to bring our whole hearts before Him, including the places where we have been wounded, disappointed, angered, and tempted to protect ourselves by becoming hardened toward the people around us.

The declaration I Refuse to Stay Bound is therefore not a claim that we have never been hurt, that we have mastered every struggle, or that we can overcome every painful experience through determination alone. It is a declaration of spiritual direction, a decision to stop allowing the failures of other people to dictate the condition of our relationship with God, and a commitment to pursue the freedom that Jesus Christ offers even when the journey toward that freedom requires honesty, repentance, patience, and the courage to face matters we would rather leave untouched. It is a declaration that our hearts belong to God and that we must be willing to let Him search us, correct us, heal us, and teach us how to respond to the wounds of life without becoming prisoners of them.

When the Wound Comes from the House of God

For many believers, some of the most confusing experiences of their lives have occurred in places where they expected to find spiritual safety, encouragement, fellowship, and truth. They entered the Church believing that the people of God would strive to love one another, that spiritual leaders would exercise their authority with humility, and that disagreements could be handled through prayer, honest conversation, and a sincere desire to honor Scripture. They understood that no congregation was perfect because every church was made up of people who needed the grace of God, yet they still hoped that the community gathering in the name of Jesus Christ would demonstrate a genuine commitment to the principles it proclaimed.

When those expectations are shattered through betrayal, manipulation, favoritism, public humiliation, false accusations, or a refusal to acknowledge wrongdoing, the resulting pain can reach beyond a broken relationship and begin to affect a person’s spiritual confidence. The individual may struggle to understand how the language of forgiveness can be preached so confidently while compassion is withheld from someone who is suffering, or how the call to humility can be applied to those who question a decision while those in authority appear unwilling to examine their own conduct. Some people eventually withdraw from fellowship because they no longer know whom they can trust, while others remain present but carry their disappointment quietly, afraid that speaking honestly about their experiences will lead to further rejection or the accusation that they are causing division.

We must be willing to acknowledge these realities without allowing them to become an excuse for condemning every church, distrusting every leader, or abandoning the faith we have placed in Jesus Christ. The failures of people who claim His name do not change His character, and the misuse of spiritual authority does not erase the truth of His Word. Nevertheless, we must not use those truths to dismiss the suffering of people who have experienced genuine harm, because the fact that Jesus Christ remains faithful does not make human wrongdoing insignificant. The Church must be willing to confront the difference between the character of Christ and the conduct of those who represent Him, recognizing that genuine spiritual maturity requires more than defending an institution or protecting a reputation when the truth calls for correction.

The apostle Paul instructed believers in Galatians 6:1 to approach the restoration of someone caught in wrongdoing with a spirit of gentleness while maintaining a careful awareness of their own vulnerability. This principle establishes a responsibility that reaches into every level of Christian life, because correction is not supposed to become an opportunity for humiliation, and restoration is not supposed to be a means of controlling someone who has already been wounded. When a person has done wrong, the biblical response must involve truth, humility, and a willingness to address the matter rather than conceal it beneath spiritual language. When someone has been hurt, the response must not automatically assume that the wounded person is bitter, rebellious, or unwilling to forgive simply because the pain has not disappeared. Both the person who caused the harm and the person who experienced it need the light of God’s Word, although their responsibilities in a particular situation may be very different.

We must also recognize that speaking the truth does not require us to speak carelessly, spread accusations without evidence, or treat every disagreement as proof of spiritual corruption. A prophetic call to the Church must be rooted in the character of God and the authority of Scripture, which means that it must confront wrongdoing without abandoning righteousness in the process. If we call for accountability, we must be willing to practice honesty ourselves; if we challenge pride in leadership, we must examine pride in our own hearts; and if we demand that others repent, we must remain willing to repent when the Lord reveals something in us that needs to change. The purpose of this book is not to create an atmosphere of suspicion or to encourage wounded believers to build their identities around the failures of others, but to call the people of God toward a deeper commitment to truth, holiness, mercy, and spiritual integrity.

The Difference Between Forgiveness and Silence

One of the greatest misunderstandings surrounding forgiveness is the assumption that a person who has truly forgiven must stop talking about what happened, restore every relationship immediately, or return to the same circumstances without asking whether anything has changed. This understanding places a burden upon the wounded that Scripture does not require, because forgiveness is not a command to deny reality, abandon wisdom, or remove every consequence of another person’s decisions. Although forgiveness calls us to release vengeance and refuse to let hatred govern our hearts, it does not make dishonesty acceptable, eliminate the need for repentance, or require us to place ourselves in situations where harmful behavior is likely to continue.

Jesus Christ taught His followers about forgiveness, mercy, and the responsibility to deal honestly with wrongdoing, and these teachings must be understood together rather than separated according to whichever principle seems most convenient in a difficult moment. In Luke 17:3 to 4, Jesus connects rebuke, repentance, and forgiveness, demonstrating that forgiveness belongs within a moral framework in which wrongdoing can be acknowledged and the need for repentance can be taken seriously. This passage does not give us permission to withhold forgiveness until another person meets every condition we establish, but it does remind us that biblical relationships are not built upon pretending that sin and its consequences do not exist. Grace does not erase truth, and truth does not require us to abandon grace.

There may be situations in which a believer chooses to forgive someone who never apologizes, acknowledges no wrongdoing, and shows no evidence of regret, because the decision to release personal vengeance before God does not have to depend upon the other person’s willingness to cooperate. In such circumstances, forgiveness can become a process of surrendering the desire to retaliate, entrusting justice to God, and refusing to let the offender remain at the center of every thought and decision. Yet the decision to forgive does not automatically restore trust, because trust concerns the reliability of a relationship and must be considered in light of honesty, consistency, accountability, and the circumstances surrounding the harm. Reconciliation, where it is possible and appropriate, involves more than one person’s private decision, and rebuilding a damaged relationship ordinarily requires the participation and responsibility of those involved.

These distinctions matter because many people have been taught to measure their forgiveness by how quickly they resume contact with someone who has hurt them, how quietly they endure repeated mistreatment, or how successfully they hide the emotional consequences of what they have experienced. Such expectations can cause people to mistake the suppression of pain for spiritual maturity, even though unaddressed wounds may continue to influence their lives in ways they do not recognize. The goal is not to keep reopening every injury or to demand that every person who has ever hurt us provide the explanation we desire, but to pursue the kind of healing that allows us to remember the truth without being controlled by the desire to punish, retaliate, or remain permanently defined by the experience.

Forgiveness is not weakness, and it is not a declaration that the offender was right. It is a response to God that allows us to release the burden of personal vengeance while continuing to act with wisdom, integrity, and appropriate boundaries. Sometimes the next step will involve a conversation, sometimes it will involve seeking help from trustworthy spiritual leaders or other appropriate sources of support, and sometimes it will involve maintaining distance from a person whose behavior remains harmful. The outward circumstances may differ, but the inward calling remains the same: we must not surrender our hearts to hatred, and we must not use the language of forgiveness to excuse conduct that needs to be confronted.

The Battle for the Heart

The most difficult part of this journey may not be recognizing that another person has done something wrong, because when the evidence of betrayal is clear, the mind can return to the details again and again in an attempt to understand what happened and why it happened. The deeper struggle begins when we discover that our response to the injury is becoming another source of bondage, shaping our conversations, influencing our relationships, and making it increasingly difficult to approach new people without expecting them to repeat the actions of those who hurt us before. We may begin to interpret every disagreement as a sign of rejection, every correction as an attack, or every unfamiliar situation as evidence that we are about to experience the same pain again. Without realizing it, we can become so occupied with guarding ourselves against another wound that we begin to organize our lives around the very thing from which we long to be free.

Hebrews 12:14 to 15 warns believers to pursue peace and holiness while remaining watchful against a root of bitterness that can grow and trouble many people. This warning deserves careful attention because bitterness rarely begins with a conscious decision to become a bitter person; it often begins with pain that has not been properly addressed, disappointment that has never been acknowledged, or a sense of injustice that continues to occupy the heart long after the original event has passed. When resentment is repeatedly rehearsed without being brought before God for honest examination, it can gradually become more than a response to what happened, developing into a way of interpreting the world and relating to other people. The danger is not that a wounded person remembers the truth, but that the injury becomes so central to their inner life that it begins to influence their character, their judgment, and their ability to receive the good that God may place before them.

This is why the call to forgiveness must include an invitation to examine our own hearts without turning that examination into another form of condemnation. God does not reveal the condition of our hearts merely to shame us for struggling, but to lead us toward repentance, healing, and a more faithful response to His Word. We must be willing to acknowledge when anger has become a desire for revenge, when discernment has become suspicion toward everyone, or when the pursuit of accountability has quietly turned into a need to see another person suffer. Such examination does not make the original wrongdoing acceptable, nor does it transfer responsibility away from the person who caused the harm. It simply recognizes that another person’s sin does not remove our own responsibility to live in obedience to God.

The freedom we seek is therefore deeper than the temporary relief of expressing anger or the satisfaction of proving that someone else was wrong. It involves allowing the Lord to transform the way we carry the experience, respond to the memory, and make decisions about our future. We may still remember what happened, and there may be consequences that require time, wisdom, and practical action, but remembrance does not have to become resentment, and pain does not have to become our permanent identity. Through prayer, Scripture, honest self-examination, appropriate support, and a willingness to obey God even when our emotions are still healing, we can begin to move toward a life in which the wound remains part of our history without ruling our hearts.

A Call to the Church and to Every Believer

Throughout these pages, we will confront uncomfortable questions about the way believers handle conflict, the way leaders exercise authority, the way churches respond to allegations of wrongdoing, and the way individuals carry offense when relationships break down. We will examine the responsibility of those who cause harm, the danger of using spiritual language to avoid accountability, and the need for congregations to practice restoration with both compassion and discernment. We will also challenge wounded believers to resist the temptation to let their pain become a justification for hatred, contempt, or the abandonment of everything they once believed about the goodness of God. These challenges must remain connected, because a call to accountability that refuses self-examination becomes hypocrisy, while a call to forgiveness that refuses to confront wrongdoing becomes a distortion of grace.

No pastor, teacher, ministry leader, or believer stands above the authority of God’s Word, and no individual should be treated as though their position makes them immune to correction. At the same time, no person should assume that their experience gives them complete understanding of every situation or removes their responsibility to speak truthfully and act justly. The Church needs people who are willing to tell the truth without using truth as a weapon, leaders who can receive correction without treating every question as rebellion, and believers who can pursue forgiveness without denying the need for accountability. Such a community will not be free from every conflict, but it can learn to respond to conflict in a manner that reflects the holiness, humility, and mercy of Jesus Christ.

Our foundation must remain in Him, because the freedom we proclaim is not a product of human determination alone but a work of God in the lives of people who are willing to submit themselves to His truth. We will return throughout this book to the words of Jesus in John 8:31 to 36, where discipleship, abiding in His Word, knowing the truth, and experiencing freedom are brought together. This passage directs our attention beyond the temporary relief of escaping a difficult relationship or situation and toward the deeper freedom that comes from belonging to Christ and allowing His Word to govern our lives. The chains we discuss will not always be visible, and they will not always be broken through one dramatic moment, but every step toward truth, repentance, forgiveness, and faithful obedience can become part of the journey toward spiritual maturity.

As you read, I encourage you to resist the temptation to approach every chapter as a message intended primarily for someone else. There may be moments when you recognize the actions of a person who hurt you, the failures of a leader you once trusted, or the shortcomings of a church that did not respond as it should have. When those moments arise, allow yourself to acknowledge the truth without permitting anger to become the only voice you hear. Then turn the same willingness to examine the truth toward your own heart, asking the Lord to reveal any resentment, pride, fear, or desire for retaliation that may be keeping you from walking in the freedom He offers. Honest self-examination does not require you to accept blame for another person’s wrongdoing; it requires you to take responsibility for your own response before God.

If you have been betrayed, I want you to know that your pain does not make you a failure in the faith, and the fact that healing takes time does not mean that God has abandoned you. If you have caused harm, I want you to understand that the path forward begins with honesty, repentance, and a willingness to accept responsibility rather than searching for spiritual explanations that allow you to avoid the consequences of your actions. If you lead a congregation, teach the Word, or serve in ministry, I want you to consider whether the people under your care can approach you with legitimate concerns and expect to be treated with fairness, dignity, and biblical integrity. And if you have found yourself caught in the cycle of offense, I want you to understand that freedom is possible without pretending that the past was harmless or that every broken relationship must return to what it once was.

The purpose of this book is not to convince you that you will never experience betrayal again, because none of us can guarantee how another person will choose to behave. The purpose is to help you become so firmly rooted in the truth of Jesus Christ that the failures of others do not determine your identity, the presence of conflict does not destroy your faith, and the struggle to forgive does not become a reason to abandon the work God desires to accomplish in your heart. We are called to walk in truth, to practice mercy, to pursue holiness, to correct wrongdoing with humility, and to remember that the Lord who calls us to forgive is also the Lord who sees every injustice and knows every hidden sorrow.

The Declaration Begins Here

The words I Refuse to Stay Bound must become more than a title printed on the cover of a book, because their meaning will be tested in the ordinary decisions we make when the memory of betrayal returns, when someone refuses to apologize, when the opportunity to retaliate appears attractive, or when we must choose between protecting our pride and obeying the Word of God. The declaration does not mean that we will never feel anger, never struggle with painful memories, or never need help from others as we move through the process of healing. It means that we will no longer treat those struggles as permission to surrender our hearts to bitterness, and we will not allow the failures of other people to become the final authority over the lives God has entrusted to us.

We will choose truth over denial, repentance over self-justification, forgiveness over personal vengeance, and wisdom over the pressure to restore relationships before trust has been rebuilt. We will refuse to confuse spiritual maturity with silence, because there are times when faithfulness requires us to speak honestly about wrongdoing and seek appropriate accountability. We will refuse to confuse boundaries with hatred, because it is possible to desire another person’s repentance and salvation while recognizing that continued closeness may not be wise or safe. Above all, we will refuse to make our wounds the center of our identity, because our lives belong to Jesus Christ, who calls us to walk in His truth and depend upon His grace.

This journey begins with a willingness to bring everything into the presence of God, not only the parts of our lives that are easy to discuss but also the memories we have tried to bury, the questions we have been afraid to ask, and the places where our own responses need to change. As we move forward, we will examine the roots of offense, the nature of forgiveness, the responsibility of repentance, the importance of accountability, and the freedom that comes from allowing the Word of God to shape our hearts. We will challenge the Church where it has failed to reflect the character of Christ, and we will challenge ourselves wherever our actions fall short of the same truth we expect others to obey.

The past may explain some of the struggles we face, but it does not have to dictate the direction of our lives. The wound may remind us of what happened, but it does not have to determine what we become. Another person may have broken our trust, but that person does not possess the authority to decide whether we will continue to grow in our relationship with God. Our responsibility is to bring our pain before the Lord, remain willing to receive His correction, and follow Him through the difficult but necessary work of forgiveness and spiritual transformation.

I refuse to stay bound, not because the wound was insignificant, but because the freedom Jesus Christ offers is greater than the power I will allow that wound to have over my life. I will not deny the truth to protect another person’s reputation, and I will not abandon the truth to justify my own bitterness. I will pursue forgiveness without excusing wrongdoing, accountability without hatred, and healing without pretending that the pain never existed. By the grace of God, I will continue to walk toward the person He has called me to become, trusting that my past does not have to rule my future and that my heart can remain His even after it has been wounded.

This is where our journey begins, with an honest acknowledgment of the pain, a renewed commitment to the truth, and a decision to seek the freedom that can only be found by walking faithfully with Jesus Christ.
