Chapter 4

The Poison of Bitterness

From I Refuse to Stay Bound by Bradley Hobbs

When the Wound Becomes a Way of Life

There is a difference between being wounded and allowing a wound to become the place from which you live. Every person who has experienced betrayal, rejection, injustice, or disappointment knows that pain can reach places in the heart that words cannot easily describe. Some wounds come from strangers whose actions we never expected to matter so deeply, while others come from the very people we trusted to protect us, pray with us, stand beside us, and help us grow. When the people who were supposed to demonstrate the love of Christ become the source of our deepest hurt, the resulting pain can challenge not only our relationships but also our understanding of God, our confidence in ourselves, and our willingness to trust again.

The danger is not that we feel pain. Pain is a human response to something that matters, and Scripture never requires us to pretend that suffering does not exist. The danger begins when pain is permitted to become the permanent authority over our thoughts, our attitudes, our decisions, and our relationships. What began as a legitimate response to wrongdoing can gradually become a lens through which we interpret every new person, every unfamiliar situation, and every opportunity for restoration. We begin to expect rejection before anyone has rejected us, prepare for betrayal before anyone has betrayed us, and defend ourselves against people who have not yet given us a reason to believe they are our enemies.

This is one of the quiet ways bitterness takes root. It rarely announces its arrival by declaring that it intends to destroy our peace. More often, it presents itself as protection. It tells us that remembering every offense will keep us from being hurt again, that refusing to trust anyone is wisdom, and that rehearsing what happened will somehow bring us closer to justice. It convinces us that holding tightly to resentment is the same thing as refusing to let the offender escape accountability. Yet while bitterness promises to protect us from future pain, it can gradually imprison us in the pain of the past.

Hebrews 12:15 warns believers to be watchful so that no root of bitterness springs up, causes trouble, and defiles many. The imagery is significant because a root is not always visible to the people standing above the ground. It develops beneath the surface, where it can grow unnoticed until its effects begin to appear. In the same way, bitterness may begin with an unspoken grievance, a painful memory, a disappointment that was never addressed, or an injustice that seemed to go unanswered. Over time, it can influence our words, shape our assumptions, harden our attitudes, and affect people who had nothing to do with the original offense.

A person may be smiling in public while privately rehearsing every detail of what happened. A church member may continue attending services while carrying resentment toward the leadership. A family may gather around the same table while old offenses quietly determine what can and cannot be discussed. A believer may speak confidently about the goodness of God while secretly wondering whether God will ever bring justice for what was done. These situations are not all identical, and unresolved pain should never be treated as proof that someone lacks faith. Nevertheless, they remind us that wounds left unattended can influence far more of our lives than we initially realize.

The question before us is not whether we have ever been hurt. The question is whether we will allow the hurt to become our master.

Bitterness Is Not the Same as Righteous Anger

Before we can confront bitterness honestly, we must understand the difference between anger that responds to wrongdoing and resentment that begins to consume the heart. Christians sometimes use the language of forgiveness so carelessly that people who have suffered genuine harm are made to feel guilty for being angry. They are told to move on before anyone has listened to them, to reconcile before the wrongdoing has been addressed, or to demonstrate grace by remaining silent about conduct that should be confronted. That is not a healthy understanding of biblical forgiveness, and it can place an unnecessary burden upon people who are already suffering.

Scripture does not teach that every expression of anger is sinful. Ephesians 4:26 instructs believers, “Be ye angry, and sin not” (KJV). The verse recognizes that anger can exist without becoming an excuse for sin. There are circumstances in which anger is an understandable response to cruelty, exploitation, dishonesty, abuse of authority, or the mistreatment of someone who cannot adequately defend themselves. When we see vulnerable people being harmed, our response should not be indifference disguised as spirituality. Love does not require us to become comfortable with injustice.

However, anger must be examined because even a legitimate emotion can lead us toward destructive choices. Anger may alert us to a boundary that has been violated, a truth that has been denied, or a person who needs protection. Bitterness begins to develop when we continually feed our resentment until the offense becomes central to our identity and the desire for justice becomes entangled with the desire to see another person suffer. Anger can move us to address wrongdoing, establish boundaries, seek help, and pursue accountability. Bitterness keeps us emotionally attached to the offense long after the immediate moment has passed, repeatedly inviting us to relive it and allowing it to govern our responses.

There is a difference between saying, “What happened to me was wrong, and it must be addressed,” and saying, “Because of what happened to me, I will never allow myself to be free again.” The first statement acknowledges reality and makes room for a responsible response. The second gives the offender’s actions continuing authority over the wounded person’s future.

We must be careful not to judge another person’s healing by the speed with which they appear to recover. Some wounds take time to process, particularly when they involve repeated mistreatment, manipulation, abuse, or a betrayal that has disrupted a person’s sense of safety. Forgiveness is not a demand to suppress grief, and healing is not a performance in which the wounded person must prove spiritual maturity by never mentioning the pain again. A person can be pursuing forgiveness while still grieving, seeking counseling, reporting misconduct, or working through the emotional consequences of what happened.

The purpose of confronting bitterness is not to silence the wounded. It is to prevent the wound from gaining control over the rest of their lives.

The Root Beneath the Surface

Bitterness often grows where pain has been combined with unanswered questions. Why did they do this to me? Why did no one intervene? Why was the truth ignored? Why did the person who caused the harm seem to move forward while I was left trying to rebuild? Why did people who knew what happened choose to protect a reputation instead of protecting the person who was suffering? These questions can become especially painful when wrongdoing occurs within a church, where believers expect to find truth, compassion, accountability, and spiritual care.

Sometimes answers are never fully provided. Sometimes the person who caused the harm refuses to acknowledge it. Sometimes an institution responds inadequately, leaving the wounded person with the additional burden of trying to make sense of the response. In such circumstances, it is understandable that the heart may struggle to release its grip on the offense. The mind continues searching for an explanation because it hopes that understanding the past will finally make the pain manageable.

Yet there are situations in which the offender will never offer an honest explanation, the people who witnessed the wrongdoing will never acknowledge what they saw, and the institution that failed to respond properly will never provide the apology the wounded person deserves. If our freedom depends entirely upon receiving the response we have been denied, then the people who hurt us continue to control the conditions of our healing.

This does not mean that answers are unimportant or that accountability should be abandoned. Truth matters. Acknowledgment matters. Appropriate consequences matter. Where wrongdoing has occurred, responsible people should take reasonable steps to address it, protect those at risk, and correct what can be corrected. But we must also recognize that another person’s refusal to do what is right does not have to become the permanent boundary of our spiritual freedom.

The Apostle Paul writes in Romans 12:19 that believers should not take vengeance into their own hands but should leave room for the judgment of God. This instruction does not call evil good, nor does it prohibit lawful action or the pursuit of justice. It confronts the temptation to appoint ourselves as the final judge and executioner of another person’s life. We can pursue appropriate accountability without surrendering our hearts to revenge. We can tell the truth without allowing hatred to dictate our conduct. We can recognize the seriousness of an offense while refusing to make another person’s downfall the condition of our own peace.

Sometimes the most difficult part of healing is accepting that we may never receive the ending we imagined. The apology may never come. The explanation may remain incomplete. The people who misunderstood us may never reconsider their conclusions. The institution that disappointed us may continue without acknowledging its failures. Those realities can be painful, but they do not mean that our lives must remain suspended until someone else decides to do the right thing.

Our healing may begin when we stop waiting for another person’s confession before we permit God to work within our own hearts.
